\begin{abstract}
\begingroup\color{revisiongreen}
Lateral vehicle control across varying speeds requires models that describe
how steering responses change with longitudinal velocity. Yet each vehicle
may supply identification data over only a narrow speed range. We develop
federated group regression to identify linear-parameter-varying (LPV) models
from these complementary observations while accounting for differences
between vehicles. Each vehicle estimates fixed-speed models from noisy state
measurements and known applied steering using output-error identification.
Clients then exchange additive statistics and model-assignment scores to
learn group-specific LPV models without transmitting trajectories.
Across ten independent simulated vehicle fleets, mean matrix error at
locally unobserved speeds is \OEFederatedUnseen\%, compared with
\OESmoothLocalUnseen\% for local identification with smoothness regularization
and \OEGlobalUnseen\% for pooling all vehicles. Diagnostics show that
accurate local estimates alone do not ensure informative groups.
Closed-loop yaw-rate tracking uses linear-quadratic-integral (LQI)
controllers designed from the identified models without further adaptation. Under noise-free state feedback,
the proposed method satisfies the tested constraints and improves mean
tracking over a fixed nominal controller. Additional gains over pooling
all vehicles are small in absolute terms and depend on the recipient.
\endgroup
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\begingroup\color{\revisioncolor}
Model-based vehicle control requires dynamics models that remain useful as
operating conditions change. For lateral motion, longitudinal speed affects
the relationship between steering, sideslip, and yaw rate. A model identified
near one operating point may therefore be inadequate elsewhere. LPV models
represent this dependence explicitly, making identification across the intended
speed range a central requirement \cite{toth2010lpv,bamieh2002}.

Obtaining that coverage separately for every vehicle is a demanding calibration
task. A vehicle may provide measurements over only a narrow part of its
intended operating range. Those measurements constrain the observed response,
but a flexible speed-dependent model can remain undetermined outside that
region. Repeating measurements at the same speeds reduces noise without
resolving missing scheduling directions. The resulting problem is therefore
incomplete operating information as well as limited sample count.

Fleet collaboration offers a way to fill these gaps: one vehicle's unobserved
speeds may be covered by others. Yet differences in mass, inertia, and tire
properties make their measurements only approximately compatible. Pooling
all vehicles can introduce bias, while separating them too aggressively can
leave each group without sufficient speed coverage. What is needed is selective
sharing that supplies missing operating information without overwhelming a
recipient's dynamics with incompatible data. Crucially, the partners must be
chosen from partial observations, before complete local models are available.

\rev{We address this need through federated LPV identification. Vehicles keep
trajectories locally, exchange additive statistics, and evaluate candidate
group models at their observed speeds. Alternating assignment and regression
learns speed-dependent models over the target interval. The central question
is whether groups inferred from partial observations can supply complementary
speed information while describing their members' dynamics accurately.}
Figure~\ref{fig:overview} summarizes the procedure.

Existing work supplies important components of this approach. Local LPV
identification constructs \rev{speed-dependent models} from operating-point
models \cite{ferranti2017}. Federated learning provides a framework for
collaboration without centralizing records \cite{mcmahan2017fedavg}, and
FedSysID characterizes the effect of dynamical heterogeneity on collaborative
identification \cite{wang2023fedsysid}. IFCA alternates cluster assignment
and model optimization \cite{ghosh2020ifca}. Clustered SysID applies this
structure to LTI systems with identical dynamics within each true cluster
\cite{toso2023clusteredsysid}. IC-SYSID adds incremental clustering and
stability promotion \cite{kececi2025clustercraft}, while
\cite{weber2026ifac} uses uncertainty-aware grouping for vehicle controller design.

\rev{Our distinction is the joint requirement of similar dynamics and
complementary speed observations. Each vehicle's observations leave its LPV
regression underdetermined, and vehicles assigned to one group need not have
identical dynamics. We adapt the established alternating clustering structure
to this setting and make the following contributions:}
\begin{enumerate}
\color{revisiongreen}
\item We formulate LPV identification from limited local speed ranges in
common physical-state coordinates, distinguishing missing speed information
from uncertainty in the fixed-speed estimates.
\item We develop a federated procedure that combines local output-error
estimates into group-specific LPV models. Additive statistics and local
assignment scores allow vehicles to select sharing partners before each
has identified its own model across the target speed range.
\item We evaluate identification at locally unobserved speeds against local
regularization and global pooling on ten independent vehicle fleets.
Closed-loop yaw-rate tracking with controllers designed from the learned
models assesses whether improved identification also benefits control.
\end{enumerate}
\rev{Sections~II and III introduce the identification problem and federated
method. Section~IV describes the benchmark and evaluation protocol.
Sections~V and VI report identification accuracy and closed-loop tracking.
Section~VII discusses the findings and limitations, and Section~VIII concludes.}
\endgroup

\section{Identification Problem}
\begingroup\color{revisiongreen}
Each vehicle needs a model across the target speed range but provides
measurements at only a few speeds. First, we identify a fixed-speed model
from each trajectory. Second, we combine these estimates into an LPV model,
using other vehicles' observations to supply missing speed information.

\subsection{Local identification from state measurements}
Consider $M$ vehicles indexed by $i\in\{1,\ldots,M\}$ and the target
speed interval $\mathcal V=[v_{\min},v_{\max}]$, where
$0<v_{\min}<v_{\max}$. Vehicle $i$ has state
$\bs{x}_i=[\beta_i,r_i]^\top\in\R^2$, comprising sideslip angle $\beta_i$
and yaw rate $r_i$, and scalar front-wheel steering input $u_i$.
Both states are measured:
$\bs{y}_{i,k}=\bs{x}_{i,k}+\bs{\nu}_{i,k}$, where $k$ indexes samples
and $\bs{\nu}_{i,k}\in\R^2$ is measurement noise.
Applied steering and longitudinal speed are assumed known.

Vehicle $i$ supplies trajectories at $n_i$ observed speeds
$\mathcal V_i=\{v_{i,1},\ldots,v_{i,n_i}\}\subset\mathcal V$.
At speed $v$, the identification segment contains $N_i(v)$ consecutive
sampling intervals, with state measurements
$\bs{y}_{i,0},\ldots,\bs{y}_{i,N_i(v)}$ and applied steering inputs
$u_{i,0},\ldots,u_{i,N_i(v)-1}$. Section~IV specifies how these data are collected.

Output-error (OE) identification estimates an affine state-space model by
comparing its simulated response with the state measurements. Suppressing
$i$ and $v$ for one vehicle--speed pair, write
\begin{equation}
 \widehat{\bs{x}}_{k+1}=\bs{A}\widehat{\bs{x}}_k+\bs{B}u_k+\bs{c},
 \qquad \widehat{\bs{x}}_0=\bs{\zeta},
 \label{eq:transition}
\end{equation}
where $\bs{A}\in\R^{2\times2}$, $\bs{B}\in\R^{2\times1}$,
$\bs{c}\in\R^2$, and $\bs{\zeta}\in\R^2$ are estimated jointly.
For measurement-noise standard deviations $\sigma_\beta,\sigma_r>0$, define
$\bs{W}=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_\beta^{-1},\sigma_r^{-1})$.
With $N=N_i(v)$, the OE objective is
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal L(\bs{A},\bs{B},\bs{c},\bs{\zeta})=
 \frac{1}{2(N+1)}\sum_{k=0}^{N}
 \|\bs{W}(\widehat{\bs{x}}_k-\bs{y}_k)\|_2^2,
 \label{eq:local}
\end{equation}
where $\|\cdot\|_2$ is the Euclidean norm and the simulated state follows
\eqref{eq:transition} without resetting to measurements.
The offset $\bs{c}$ avoids requiring a known equilibrium center, and
$\bs{\zeta}$ describes the state at the start of the identification segment,
after the vehicle has approached steady cornering.

The estimated matrices $\widehat{\bs{A}}_i(v)$ and
$\widehat{\bs{B}}_i(v)$ form the row vector
$\widetilde{\bs{m}}_i(v)\in\R^{1\times6}$, ordered as
$[\widehat A_{i,11},\widehat A_{i,12},\widehat A_{i,21},
\widehat A_{i,22},\widehat B_{i,1},\widehat B_{i,2}]$.
They supply the scheduling dataset
$\mathcal D_i=\{(v,\widetilde{\bs{m}}_i(v)):v\in\mathcal V_i\}$.
The estimated offsets and initial states remain local.

\subsection{Missing speed information and group models}
Let $\bs{\phi}(v)\in\R^L$ contain $L$ linearly independent scheduling
functions shared by all vehicles. Define
$\bs{\Phi}_i\in\R^{n_i\times L}$ and $\bs{Y}_i\in\R^{n_i\times6}$
with rows $\bs{\phi}(v_{i,\ell})^\top$ and
$\widetilde{\bs{m}}_i(v_{i,\ell})$, respectively.
The setting of interest has
\begin{equation}
 \rank\bs{\Phi}_i<L.
 \label{eq:local-deficiency}
\end{equation}
Then some nonzero $\bs{U}_i\in\R^{L\times6}$ satisfies
$\bs{\Phi}_i\bs{U}_i=\bs{0}$. Adding $\bs{U}_i$ to a coefficient
matrix leaves predictions at observed speeds unchanged but can change
predictions elsewhere. Additional samples at the same speeds cannot remove
this ambiguity. Exciting the dynamics at a fixed speed and observing their
variation with speed are therefore separate requirements.

For $K$ group models, let $g_i\in\{1,\ldots,K\}$ assign vehicle $i$ to
a group with coefficients $\bs{\Theta}_{g_i}\in\R^{L\times6}$.
To describe approximation error, let $\bs{m}_i^\star(v)$ collect the
vehicle's linearized dynamic matrices, defined for evaluation in Section~IV.
Write
\begin{equation}
 \bs{m}_i^\star(v)=\bs{\phi}(v)^\top\bs{\Theta}_{g_i}+\bs{d}_i(v).
 \label{eq:map}
\end{equation}
The row vector $\bs{d}_i(v)\in\R^{1\times6}$ accounts for basis
approximation and differences between a vehicle and its group model.
Pooling can supply missing speed information, but this mismatch can reduce
the benefit for individual vehicles. It is distinct from errors in the
local OE estimates caused by measurement noise, finite-amplitude motion,
and numerical optimization.

\subsection{Identification objective}
Given the local datasets, we seek $K$, assignments $g_i$, and coefficients
$\widehat{\bs{\Theta}}_g$ giving
$\widehat{\bs{m}}_i(v)=\bs{\phi}(v)^\top\widehat{\bs{\Theta}}_{g_i}$
across $\mathcal V$. Clients exchange the statistics and scores defined
below. True vehicle parameters, reference matrices, and physical family
labels are excluded from fitting and grouping. Each vehicle contributes
its observed speeds, and evaluation concerns predictions at its locally
unobserved speeds. The target domain is the prescribed speed interval along
a cornering condition, rather than the full nonlinear state--input domain.
\endgroup

\section{\rev{Federated Learning of Group-Specific LPV Models}}
\begingroup\color{revisiongreen}
Using $\bs{Y}_i$ and $\bs{\Phi}_i$, we alternate group-model regression
and vehicle assignment based on agreement at observed speeds.
A complexity-penalized residual score selects the group count.
We call this procedure \GroupedFL{}. It selects sharing partners from
partial observations; group coverage is assessed afterward rather than enforced.

\subsection{Group regression from additive statistics}
Given the current assignments, define the vehicles in group $g$ as
$\mathcal I_g=\{i:g_i=g\}$. Their coefficient matrix minimizes
\begin{equation}
 \widehat{\bs{\Theta}}_g=\arg\min_{\bs{\Theta}\in\R^{L\times6}}
 \left\{\sum_{i\in\mathcal I_g}\|\bs{Y}_i-\bs{\Phi}_i\bs{\Theta}\|_F^2
       +\rho\|\bs{\Theta}\|_F^2\right\},
 \label{eq:group-objective}
\end{equation}
where $\|\cdot\|_F$ is the Frobenius norm and $\rho>0$ is a common ridge
parameter. Each speed--matrix target has equal weight. Client $i$ computes
$\bs{G}_i=\bs{\Phi}_i^\top\bs{\Phi}_i\in\R^{L\times L}$ and
$\bs{H}_i=\bs{\Phi}_i^\top\bs{Y}_i\in\R^{L\times6}$.
The server sums these statistics and solves
\begin{equation}
 \left(\sum_{i\in\mathcal I_g}\bs{G}_i+\rho\bs{I}_L\right)
 \widehat{\bs{\Theta}}_g=\sum_{i\in\mathcal I_g}\bs{H}_i,
 \label{eq:fed}
\end{equation}
where $\bs{I}_L$ is the identity matrix. For the same assignments, this is
the centralized ridge-regression solution in exact arithmetic, without
transmitting the local targets $\bs{Y}_i$.

Let $\bs{\Phi}_g$ stack the designs of the group members. The unregularized
fit is unique exactly when $\rank\bs{\Phi}_g=L$, since its Gram matrix
is $\bs{\Phi}_g^\top\bs{\Phi}_g$. Thus complementary observations can
resolve missing scheduling directions. Ridge regularization gives a unique
solution even without full rank, but does not add information. Conditioning,
local estimation error, and within-group mismatch also affect prediction accuracy.

\subsection{Vehicle assignment and group-count selection}
Assignment compares each candidate model with a vehicle's observed dynamics.
A positive diagonal matrix $\bs{D}\in\R^{6\times6}$ balances the six
entries using their fleet-wide standard deviations, with a small common
floor. It is computed from local target moments and fixed during the search.
Unlike the OE weights $\bs{W}$, it describes variation across vehicles.
The implementation notes specify the scaling rule.
After receiving the current group coefficients, client $i$ returns
\begin{equation}
 q_{ig}=\|(\bs{Y}_i-\bs{\Phi}_i\widehat{\bs{\Theta}}_g)
             \bs{D}^{-1}\|_F^2,\quad g=1,\ldots,K.
 \label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
The lowest score determines its assignment, with ties resolved by the
lowest group index. Regression uses unscaled errors whereas assignment
uses scaled errors. The alternating procedure is therefore heuristic,
without a common-objective descent guarantee.

Initialization first fits one model to all vehicles, denoted \GlobalModel{},
with coefficients $\widehat{\bs{\Theta}}_0$. Client $i$ sends its mean residual
\begin{equation}
 \bs{a}_i=\frac{1}{n_i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_i}
 \left[\widetilde{\bs{m}}_i(v_{i,\ell})-
 \bs{\phi}(v_{i,\ell})^\top\widehat{\bs{\Theta}}_0\right].
 \label{eq:initial-summary}
\end{equation}
The server standardizes residual entries across clients and applies K-means,
using only observed-speed information. Constant entries use unit scale.

For residual sum $\mathrm{SSE}=\sum_i q_{i,g_i}$ and scalar target count
$n=6\sum_i n_i$, the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) based score is
\begin{equation}
 \mathrm{BIC}=n\log\!\left(\max\{\mathrm{SSE}/n,10^{-12}\}\right)
                 +p_K\log n,
 \label{eq:bic}
\end{equation}
where $p_K=6LK+K-1$ counts model coefficients and a nominal mixture-weight
term. This is a heuristic complexity penalty: mixture weights are not
estimated, and OE target entries need not be independent.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:federated-lpv} selects the lowest-scoring candidate,
including \GlobalModel{} at $K=1$. A minimum group size limits small groups
but does not guarantee sufficient speed coverage.

 }

\subsection{Information exchanged}
Clients send $\bs{G}_i\in\R^{L\times L}$ and
$\bs{H}_i\in\R^{L\times6}$: 28 distinct symmetric entries and 42 entries,
respectively, for $L=7$. Initialization adds target moments and residuals.
Assignment exchanges $K$ scores per client and $K$ models of $6L$ coefficients.
Message size is independent of trajectory length, while total communication
depends on the search budget. Trajectories and targets remain local in the
emulator, but shared statistics provide no formal privacy guarantee.
\endgroup

\section{Benchmark and Evaluation}
We test whether selective sharing improves identification at locally
unobserved speeds. Every comparison method receives the same local OE
estimates, so differences reflect sharing and extrapolation rather than
different local estimators. Settings were fixed after development on three
pilot fleets and then applied to ten independently generated evaluation fleets.

\subsection{Vehicle fleet and local speed coverage}
The nonlinear single-track plant has state $\bs{x}=[\beta,r]^\top$,
with sideslip measured at the center of mass, and steering input $u$.
At fixed longitudinal speed $v$, its dynamics are
\begin{align}
 \dot\beta&=(F_f+F_r)/(mv)-r,\\
 \dot r&=(l_fF_f-l_rF_r)/I_z,\\
 F_a&=\mu F_{za}\tanh(C_a\alpha_a/(\mu F_{za})),\quad a\in\{f,r\}.
\end{align}
Here $m$ is mass, $I_z$ is yaw inertia, and $l_f,l_r$ are the distances
from the center of mass to the front/rear axles. For axle $a$, $F_a$,
$F_{za}$, $C_a$, and $\alpha_a$ denote lateral force, normal load,
cornering stiffness, and slip angle, with
$\alpha_f=u-\beta-l_fr/v$, $\alpha_r=-\beta+l_rr/v$,
$F_{zf}=mg l_r/(l_f+l_r)$, and $F_{zr}=mg l_f/(l_f+l_r)$.
Gravity is $g=9.81$ m/s$^2$ and friction coefficient $\mu=0.9$.
Angles are in radians and yaw rate in rad/s.

Each fleet contains 30 vehicles, ten from each parameter center in
Table~\ref{tab:centers}. Mass and inertia have independent uniform scatter
of $\pm2.5\%$, axle stiffnesses $\pm5\%$, and geometry is fixed.
The target interval is $\mathcal V=[10,30]$ m/s. Local speed blocks
$\{10,12,14\}$, $\{16,18,20\}$, $\{22,24,26\}$, and $\{28,30\}$ m/s
are randomly assigned to eight, eight, seven, and seven vehicles,
without using family labels. Thus 23 vehicles observe three speeds and
seven observe two, giving $23\cdot3+7\cdot2=83$ vehicle--speed datasets per fleet.

\subsection{Identification trajectories and local estimation}
\label{sec:calibration}
At each observed speed, a 44-s maneuver starts at $\beta=r=0$.
One nominal LTI model, linearized at straight driving and 20 m/s using
the Nominal parameter center, supplies an LQI controller for all vehicles
and speeds. The state--integral weights are
$\bs{Q}=\operatorname{diag}(25,50,1000)$ and the steering weight is
$R_u=0.15$. The integral of measured yaw-rate error is bounded by
$\pm0.6$ rad with conditional anti-windup. Steering is limited to
$\pm12^\circ$ and $30^\circ$/s.

During 0--4 s, the yaw-rate reference increases smoothly from zero to
$v\kappa_0$, where $\kappa_0=0.005$ m$^{-1}$ is the target curvature.
The reference remains constant until 24 s, allowing the vehicle to approach
steady cornering. Only the final 20 s are used for identification.
In this segment, with local time $\tau=t-24$ s, the reference is
\begin{equation}
 r^{\rm ref}(\tau)=v\kappa_0+\frac{a_{\rm exc}}{v}h(\tau),
 \qquad a_{\rm exc}=0.3~\mathrm{m/s^2}.
 \label{eq:acquisition-reference}
\end{equation}
The unit-peak waveform $h$ sums sinusoids at 0.2, 0.5, 0.9, and 1.4 Hz
with reproducibly randomized phases. Two-second sine-squared tapers switch
the variation smoothly on and off. A separate validation maneuver replaces
the multisine by a 0.15--1.2 Hz chirp. The LQI controller computes steering
from the reference and measured states; sideslip follows the vehicle dynamics.

At sample time $T_s=0.01$ s, the 20-s identification segment contains
$N_i(v)=2000$ intervals: 2001 state measurements and 2000 applied inputs.
Measurement noise is independent zero-mean Gaussian across samples and
components, with standard deviations $0.01^\circ$ for sideslip and
$0.03^\circ$/s for yaw rate. Both feedback and OE use these noisy state
measurements. OE uses the known applied steering, without input measurement
noise. RK4 integrates the nonlinear dynamics with steering held within each step.
OE minimizes~\eqref{eq:local} using four initializations and selects the
converged solution with the lowest training loss, without imposing stability
on the final estimate. The validation trajectory is not used to select the fit.

\subsection{LPV representation and comparison methods}
\label{sec:baselines}
The common basis combines inverse-speed terms from the bicycle model with
polynomial terms that allow additional variation around cornering:
\begin{equation}
 \bs{b}(v)=[1,v^{-1},v^{-2},z,z^2,z^3,z^4]^\top,
 \quad z=(v-20)/10.
 \label{eq:basis}
\end{equation}
A fixed QR transformation on a dense speed grid defines the normalized
basis $\bs{\phi}(v)$, improving conditioning without changing its span.
Earlier blocked-speed validation on development reference matrices selected
these seven functions for cornering and $[1,v^{-1},v^{-2}]$ for straight
driving (Fig.~\ref{fig:basis-information}(a)). The one-standard-error
selection rule used a relative-error floor of $10^{-4}$.

The comparisons test three alternatives to selective sharing.
\RestrictedLocal{} asks whether a simpler speed dependence suffices:
it fits the three straight-driving functions to each vehicle's own estimates
with ridge $10^{-8}$. \GlobalModel{} tests whether sharing all observations
is sufficient, fitting one seven-function model to the complete fleet.
\SmoothLocal{} tests whether smoothness can replace collaboration,
using the same seven-function basis but only the recipient's own observations:
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \widehat{\bs{\Theta}}_i^{\rm S}=\arg\min_{\bs{\Theta}}\Bigl\{&
 \frac{\|\bs{Y}_i-\bs{\Phi}_i\bs{\Theta}\|_F^2}{n_i}\\
 &+\alpha\|\bs{Q}_2\bs{\Theta}\|_F^2+
 \eta\|\bs{\Theta}\|_F^2\Bigr\}.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:smooth-local}
\end{equation}
Here $\bs{Q}_2$ implements the integrated squared second derivative of
the matrix functions with respect to normalized speed $z$.
It penalizes curvature while leaving affine functions unpenalized;
$\eta=10^{-16}$ is numerical ridge. Development selected
$\alpha=10^6$ as the largest penalty within one standard error of the best
mean unobserved-speed error. All seven tested values
$\{10^{-6},10^{-4},10^{-2},1,10^2,10^4,10^6\}$ are also evaluated in a
sensitivity analysis, without changing the primary setting.

\GroupedFL{} follows Section~III with $K_{\max}=5$, $N_{\rm start}=12$,
$T_{\max}=30$, $M_{\min}=5$, and $\rho=10^{-8}+10^{-12}$.
These fixed search budgets allow multiple initializations and early stopping
at unchanged assignments. Numerical details are recorded in the accompanying
implementation notes.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics and statistical comparisons}
Reference matrices are obtained from the known nonlinear simulation model.
At each speed, we find steady cornering with yaw rate $r_i^\star=v\kappa_0$,
linearize the dynamics with respect to state and steering, and discretize
the resulting matrices at $T_s$ using a zero-order hold. Their entries form
$\bs{m}_i^\star(v)$ in the same order as the estimates.
True recipient parameters are used for simulation and evaluation only,
not for OE identification, grouping, or the nominal data-collection controller.

On $\mathcal V_{\rm grid}=\{10,12,\ldots,30\}$ m/s, define relative
matrix error and mean unobserved-speed error for fleet $s$ as
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 e_i(v)&=\frac{\|\widehat{\bs{m}}_i(v)-\bs{m}_i^\star(v)\|_2}
                    {\|\bs{m}_i^\star(v)\|_2},\\
 E_s^{\rm U}&=\frac1M\sum_{i=1}^M\frac1{|\mathcal U_i|}
                    \sum_{v\in\mathcal U_i}e_i(v),
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:error}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal U_i=\mathcal V_{\rm grid}\setminus\mathcal V_i$ and $M=30$.
Speeds are averaged within vehicles, vehicles equally within fleets, and
fleets equally across the experiment. Observed-speed and full-grid errors
use $\mathcal V_i$ and $\mathcal V_{\rm grid}$ instead.
Separate $\bs{A}$, $\bs{B}$, and entrywise errors test whether gains extend
beyond the combined, state-unit-dependent metric.

For each primary baseline $b\in\{\text{\SmoothLocal{}},\text{\GlobalModel{}}\}$,
use the paired fleet difference
$\Delta_s^{(b)}=100(E_s^{{\rm U},b}-E_s^{{\rm U},{\rm GF}})$
in percentage points. Two Bonferroni-adjusted 97.5\% two-sided paired-$t$
intervals provide at least 95\% simultaneous coverage under the paired-$t$
assumptions. Fleets are the independent sampling units. Improvement requires both lower
bounds above zero. Mean paired percentage gains are reported descriptively.

\subsection{Trajectory checks and diagnostic comparisons}
Before comparing models, training and validation trajectories are checked
against steering bounds, $|\beta|\leq5^\circ$, and $|a_y|\leq6$ m/s$^2$.
For the fixed-speed bicycle model,
$a_y=(F_f+F_r)/m=v(\dot\beta+r)$.
Yaw-rate error and state drift over the final 2 s before identification
must indicate steady cornering. Limiter activation is allowed because OE
uses the applied steering. Failed fits or trajectory checks invalidate the
fleet condition; no vehicles are removed or replaced.
Centralized regression with the same assignments verifies the federated
calculation to tolerance $10^{-8}$.

Two secondary comparisons test whether accurate local estimates or known
vehicle families alone ensure useful sharing. Clean-state OLS uses exact
simulator states and applied inputs from the same noisy-feedback trajectories,
centered using the preceding quiet segment. Known-family pooling uses the
physical labels instead of learned assignments. Neither changes the primary
method. Group rank and conditioning help interpret their prediction errors.

\section{Identification Results}
\begingroup\color{revisiongreen}
We first assess the local estimates and available speed information.
We then compare prediction at unobserved speeds and examine the learned groups.
Sensitivity checks test the local prior and error metric.
Finally, coverage failures identify limits of sharing.

\subsection{Local estimates and complementary speed observations}
All $10\cdot83=830$ vehicle--speed OE fits converge, and all identification
maneuvers satisfy the trajectory and settling checks.
Local matrix error has median \OELocalMedian\% and 95th percentile
\OELocalPninetyfive\%. The steering limits introduced in

For the same group assignments, centralized and federated regression agree
within $\OECoefficientDelta$ in coefficients and $\OEPredictionDelta$ in predictions.

\subsection{When group coverage limits prediction}
Accurate local estimates need not produce informative groups.
Clean-state OLS reduces median local matrix error to \OECleanLocalMedian\%,
yet in one fleet the selected partition contains a five-vehicle group
observing only 10--20 m/s. Its design has rank six
(Fig.~\ref{fig:basis-information}(b)), and its unobserved-speed error is
\OEDeficientGroupUnseen\%, raising the fleet mean to \OECleanFailureUnseen\%.
Because both local targets and assignments change, this example identifies
a coverage failure rather than isolating the effect of measurement noise.

Known-family pooling also fails when a physical family lacks observations
at 10--14 m/s: two fleets have mean unobserved-speed errors of
\OEFamilyWorst\% and \OEFamilySecondWorst\%. Their group designs are full
rank but have condition numbers above 3000 in the same basis.
These cases explain why similarity alone is insufficient: sharing partners
must also supply enough independent, well-conditioned speed information.

\section{\rev{Closed-Loop Yaw-Rate Tracking with Identified Models}}
\begingroup\color{revisiongreen}
We assess whether the identified models support useful controller design.
The same 300 evaluation vehicles now perform tracking maneuvers without
further model fitting or controller-weight tuning. \NominalLTI{} tests
improvement over the data-collection controller, and \GlobalModel{} tests
the additional benefit of selective sharing.

\subsection{Common controller design}
The four identification methods supply scheduled LQI controllers.
A privileged \ReferenceLPV{} model fits the seven-function basis to each
recipient's full reference grid. It provides a comparison, not a performance
bound. \NominalLTI{} keeps the original 20-m/s gain and prefilter.
Scheduled methods synthesize gains at 10, 12, \ldots, 30 m/s.

At each node, the identified dynamic matrices define
\begin{equation}
 \bs{A}_{\rm a}=\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\bs{A}}&\bs{0}\\T_s\bs{c}_r&1\end{bmatrix},
 \quad \bs{B}_{\rm a}=\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\bs{B}}\\0\end{bmatrix},
 \quad \bs{c}_r=[0,1].
 \label{eq:lqi-augmentation}
\end{equation}
Discrete Riccati synthesis uses the weights from Section~\ref{sec:calibration}
and yields $\bs{K}=[\bs{K}_x,k_\xi]\in\R^{1\times3}$ for state
$[\beta,r,\xi]^\top$, where $\xi$ integrates yaw-rate error.
For zero-integral equilibrium of the fitted model at unit yaw reference, solve
\begin{equation}
 (\bs{I}_2-\widehat{\bs{A}})\bs{h}-\widehat{\bs{B}}w=\bs{0},
 \quad \bs{c}_r\bs{h}=1,\quad F=w+\bs{K}_x\bs{h},
 \label{eq:prefilter}
\end{equation}
with $\bs{h}\in\R^2$ and scalar $w$. Then $\bs{x}_{\rm eq}=\bs{h}\bar r$ and $u_{\rm eq}=w\bar r$
satisfy the model and feedback law at constant reference $\bar r$ and $\xi=0$.
Gains $\bs{K}$ and scalar prefilters $F$ are linearly interpolated between nodes.
Synthesis uses the dynamic matrices, while OE offsets and initial states
remain parameters of the local identification problems.

For yaw-rate reference $r_k^{\rm ref}$, the command and candidate integral are
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 u_k^\circ&=-\bs{K}(v_k)[\beta_k,r_k,\xi_k]^\top+F(v_k)r_k^{\rm ref},\\
 \xi_{k+1}^{\rm cand}&=\operatorname{clip}_{[-0.6,0.6]}
 \{\xi_k+T_s(r_k-r_k^{\rm ref})\}.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:lqi-runtime}
\end{equation}
Steering is rate-limited relative to $u_{k-1}$, then amplitude-limited.
Conditional anti-windup accepts the integral update only when the command
is unsaturated or the increment counteracts limiting.
Unlike identification, these control tests use noise-free state feedback
to isolate the consequences of the learned models.

\subsection{Tracking at locally unobserved speeds}
Separate 12-s runs at $v=13,21,29$ m/s test interpolation between synthesis
nodes at speeds unobserved locally by every vehicle.
Each starts at $\bs{x}_0=\bs{0}$, $\xi_0=0$, $u_{-1}=0$, with reference
\begin{equation}
 r^{\rm ref}(t)=\frac{2.5}{v}\sin^3\!\left(\frac{\pi(t-2)}{4}\right),
 \qquad 2\leq t\leq10,
 \label{eq:transient-reference}
\end{equation}
and zero otherwise, where time is in seconds. Peak nominal lateral demand
is $2.5$ m/s$^2$. RK4 uses $T_s=0.01$ s with steering held within each step.
Common bounds are $12^\circ$ steering, $120^\circ$/s steering rate, and
$6^\circ$ sideslip. The rate bound is higher than the $30^\circ$/s limit
used for identification. Limiting and bound violations are recorded separately.
Full-duration yaw RMSE includes finite responses that violate bounds,
with equal vehicle and fleet weighting.

\subsection{Tracking under varying speed}
Two supporting 12-s stress tests prescribe $11\to29\to15$ and
$29\to12\to28$ m/s, with peak nominal lateral demand up to $7.7$ m/s$^2$.
\GroupedFL{} satisfies the bounds in all 600 runs.
Mean paired fleet-relative gains over \NominalLTI{} are
\OECtrlNominalModerateGain\% and \OECtrlNominalHardGain\%, versus
\OECtrlGlobalModerateGain\% and \OECtrlGlobalHardGain\% over \GlobalModel{}.
In these stress tests, all 100 heavy-family vehicles improve over
\GlobalModel{} and all 100 handling-family vehicles worsen.
The control benefit therefore depends on the maneuver and recipient,
even when fleet-average identification improves.

\section{\rev{Discussion}}
\begingroup\color{revisiongreen}
The results support sharing observations that are both dynamically similar
and complementary in speed. Local accuracy alone does not resolve missing
scheduling directions, and physical family membership does not ensure a
well-conditioned group regression. The current assignment procedure does
not enforce these information requirements. Recipient-level coverage and
conditioning checks are therefore important before using a shared model.

Closed-loop gains over \GlobalModel{} are small and recipient-dependent.
The tests use simulated vehicles, common controller weights, and noise-free
state feedback.
Prescribed-speed lateral dynamics omit longitudinal tire forces, braking
load transfer, and combined slip, so the variable-speed tests are scheduling
stress tests rather than validation of complete maneuver feasibility.
Nonlinear and varying-speed stability, observer-based control, and full path
tracking remain untested. All vehicles contribute local data, so transfer
to new vehicles requires separate evaluation.
\endgroup

\section{\rev{Conclusion}}
\begingroup\color{revisiongreen}
Federated group regression combines fixed-speed OE estimates from vehicles
with limited local speed ranges into group-specific LPV models.
Across ten independent simulated fleets, mean error at locally unobserved
speeds is \OEFederatedUnseen\%, compared with \OESmoothLocalUnseen\%
for \SmoothLocal{} and \OEGlobalUnseen\% for \GlobalModel{}.
Controllers designed from the learned models improve mean yaw-rate tracking
over a fixed nominal controller and satisfy the tested constraints in all
1500 grouped-model runs. Future work should assess measured vehicle data
and develop recipient-level checks for harmful transfer before controller deployment.
\endgroup
