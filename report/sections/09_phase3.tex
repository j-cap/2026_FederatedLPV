\section{Phase 3: linear-plant controller oracle}
\label{sec:phase3}

\subsection{Experiment 3A: common controller architecture}
\paragraph{Objective.}
Translate model differences into a fair closed-loop comparison.

\paragraph{Setup.}
Augment the state with yaw-rate tracking error,
\begin{equation}
 \chi_{k+1}=\chi_k+T_s(r_k-r_k^\star),
\end{equation}
and use
\begin{equation}
 \delta_k=-K_x(v_k)x_k-K_I(v_k)\chi_k+k_r(v_k)r_k^\star.
\end{equation}
For LPV methods, calculate LQI gains at
$v\in\{10,15,20,25,30\}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and interpolate them. Select common
$Q$ and $R$ once on the nominal vehicle and freeze them.

\paragraph{Planned evidence and decision gate.}
Audit controllability and closed-loop spectral radius for every client on a dense
speed grid. Gain interpolation must be continuous and all evaluated closed loops
must satisfy the predefined stability margin before simulation.

\paragraph{Results, interpretation, and decision.}
The common design uses
\begin{equation}
 Q=\operatorname{diag}(100,10,500),\qquad R=10,
\end{equation}
selected once and held fixed for M1--M5.  For a unit constant yaw-rate target,
the design model is solved for $(x^\star,\delta^\star)$, and the prefilter is
\begin{equation}
 k_r=\delta^\star+K_xx^\star.
\end{equation}
This makes $(x^\star,\chi^\star=0)$ consistent with the full feedback law.  All
augmented design pairs are controllable.  M3 and M4 gains are linearly
interpolated over the five controller speeds, while M5 deliberately retains
nearest-prototype switching.

An implementation audit caught and rejected an initial prefilter
$k_r=1/G_r(1)$ that supplied only equilibrium steering and omitted
$K_xx^\star$.  That version forced the integrator to reconstruct the missing
feedback command and reversed the expected tracking ranking.  The correction
above is the standard equilibrium-consistent 2-DOF construction and was applied
identically to every method before accepting any Phase-3 result.  It is not
method-specific retuning.

The frozen-grid audit covers all 300 clients, five methods, and 201 speeds.  All
closed loops are stable.  The worst spectral radius is 0.969 for M4, leaving a
minimum frozen-model margin of 0.031.  Interpolated gains are continuous by
construction and tested at grid boundaries.  Experiment 3A therefore passes.

\subsection{Experiment 3B: closed-loop scenarios C1--C4}
\paragraph{Objective.}
Test scheduling, specialization, their combination, and out-of-distribution
generalization in closed loop.

\paragraph{Predeclared expectation.}
LPV methods dominate for a fixed family under changing speed (C1), specialized
methods dominate across families at fixed speed (C2), M4 performs best when both
effects vary (C3), and its advantage survives unseen speed--maneuver combinations
(C4).

\paragraph{Setup and planned evidence.}
Use a smooth yaw-rate sine, single lane change, and double lane change/S-curve.
Measure yaw-rate RMSE, sideslip RMS, steering RMS, peak steering, peak steering
rate, worst-family result, and family gap. Plot one representative varying-speed
trajectory and aggregate distributions across clients and seeds.

\paragraph{Decision gate.}
M4 must improve tracking or family consistency without materially greater steering
effort. Benefits must survive C4. A method that wins only through aggressive input
or one favorable maneuver does not pass.

\paragraph{Results, interpretation, and decision.}
The four scenarios use independent yaw-rate references and speed profiles.  C1
uses the nominal family, a smooth sine reference, and
$12\rightarrow28\rightarrow16\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.  C2 uses all families at
$20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ with a lane-change reference.  C3 combines all
families with the three Phase-1 speed profiles.  C4 pairs the unseen
$28\rightarrow16\rightarrow24\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ sequence with a
different-amplitude and different-timing S-curve.  Table~\ref{tab:experiment-3}
reports aggregate tracking and the C3 effort/stability diagnostics.

The intended factor isolation is visible in closed loop.  In C1, adding
scheduling reduces M1's tracking error by 45.2\% for M3.  In C2, family
specialization reduces M3's error by 17.2\% for M4.  In the combined C3 test,
M4 improves on M2 by 40.7\% and on M3 by 21.7\%.  All improvements occur for
10/10 fleet seeds.  M4 also reduces the C3 family gap from
$0.00682$ to $0.000849\,\mathrm{deg\,s^{-1}}$ relative to M3, an 87.6\%
reduction.

These tracking gains do not come from greater control effort.  M3 and M4 have
nearly identical C3 steering RMS, peak steering, and peak steering rate.  M4
also improves on M5's C3 tracking by 11.9\%.  Although M5 has similar steering
RMS, nearest-grid gain switching raises its worst peak steering rate to
$28.0\,\mathrm{deg\,s^{-1}}$, compared with $1.25\,\mathrm{deg\,s^{-1}}$ for
M4.  This is a concrete practical advantage of a continuous controller family
over discrete prototypes.

The C4 result supports a narrower generalization claim.  M4 improves on global
LPV M3 by 13.4\% for every seed and reduces its family gap by approximately
77\%, so specialization remains useful on the unseen combination.  However,
clustered LTI M2 is 3.76\% better than M4 on mean C4 tracking for all ten seeds.
Thus M4 is not universally optimal on every trajectory, and this negative
comparison is retained rather than hidden.  The particular C4 sequence does not
reward scheduling enough to overcome that small difference.

Experiment 3B is therefore a qualified pass.  The central complementarity claim
holds strongly in C1--C3 and survives C4 relative to the appropriate global LPV
baseline, with unchanged control effort and improved family consistency.  The
data do not support the stronger claim that clustered LPV must beat clustered
LTI on every individual speed--maneuver combination.  Phase 4 should now test
the LTI-complexity tradeoff systematically rather than relying on a single
five-point grid.
