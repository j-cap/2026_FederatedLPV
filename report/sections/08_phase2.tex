\section{Phase 2: oracle model comparison}
\label{sec:phase2}
This is the first direct test of the central hypothesis. Family labels are known,
data are long and low-noise, and learning imperfections are intentionally absent.

\subsection{Experiment 2A: construct M1--M5}
\paragraph{Objective.}
Construct the architectures in \cref{tab:methods} under matched data support.

\paragraph{Setup and fairness rules.}
Global models pool all clients. Clustered models use true family labels. LTI
models are fitted across the same operating data as LPV models rather than at one
favorable speed. Aggregation is uniform initially. Uncertainty weighting and
learned clusters remain outside the oracle comparison.

\paragraph{Decision gate.}
All methods must pass numerical checks and use identical train/test splits. Model
errors are decomposed by method, speed, family, client, and trajectory.

\paragraph{Results, interpretation, and decision.}
M1--M5 were constructed independently for each of the ten fleet seeds.  Each
seed contains 30 clients, ten from each oracle family.  Every architecture uses
the same exact ZOH matrices at the five Phase-1 fitting anchors.  M1 averages
over clients and speed, M2 averages over speed within each family, M3 fits one
pooled reciprocal LPV model, and M4 fits one reciprocal LPV model per family.
M5 averages clients within each family and anchor, then selects the nearest of
the resulting 15 frozen prototypes at evaluation time.  All required matrix
dimensions, family assignments, model counts, and prediction interfaces pass
automated tests.  Experiment 2A therefore passes without changing the
predeclared architectures or support.

\subsection{Experiment 2B: isolate scheduling and specialization}
\paragraph{Objective.}
Determine whether scheduling and structural specialization solve distinct and
complementary errors.

\paragraph{Predeclared expectation.}
For speed variation within one family, LPV models outperform LTI models. At fixed
speed across families, specialized models outperform global models. With both
effects present, M4 outperforms M2 and M3.

\paragraph{Setup and planned evidence.}
Three diagnostic conditions are used:
\begin{enumerate}
 \item D1: one family over the full speed envelope;
 \item D2: all families at $v_x=20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$;
 \item D3: all families under varying speed.
\end{enumerate}
Report one-step and free-run errors, error-versus-speed curves, worst-family error,
and family gap. After development, repeat with ten independently sampled fleets.

\paragraph{Decision gate.}
M4 must improve on both M2 and M3 in D3. A useful working gate is more than 10\%
for LPV relevance under broad speed variation and approximately 5--10\% for
specialization, with practical scale and seed consistency taking precedence over
the numerical thresholds. If M3 and M4 are equivalent, clustering is unnecessary.
If M2 and M4 are equivalent, LPV scheduling is unnecessary.

\paragraph{Results and decision.}
Evaluation uses a 201-point speed grid over
$[10,30]\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and the three independent varying-speed profiles
from Experiment 1B.  The trajectory plant is the exact client-specific
speed-scheduled ZOH model.  Table~\ref{tab:experiment-2} aggregates 300 clients,
60,300 client--speed evaluations per method, and 900 trajectories per method.
All errors are relative percentages.  The family gap is the difference between
the largest and smallest family-mean free-run errors in percentage points.

The complementary effects are summarized visually in \cref{fig:experiment-2}
and numerically in \cref{tab:experiment-2}.

The three diagnostics support the intended two-factor interpretation.  Under
varying speed, M3 reduces mean free-run error by 33.1\% relative to M1.  When
family specialization is already present, M4 improves on M2 by 73.0\%.  At the
fixed $20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ diagnostic, M4 reduces matrix error relative to
M3 by 64.8\%, so the specialization effect is not an artifact of changing
speed.  Finally, M4 improves varying-speed free-run error over M3 by 67.5\%.
Thus, neither scheduling nor clustering subsumes the other.

These effects are seed-consistent.  M1$\rightarrow$M3 improves all ten fleets
by 28.1--36.9\%, M2$\rightarrow$M4 by 69.3--76.3\%, and
M3$\rightarrow$M4 by 62.0--71.3\%.  M4 also reduces the family gap from 6.85
to 1.06 percentage points.  Its remaining error is primarily the deliberately
retained within-family client scatter, since one family model is not expected
to reproduce every individual vehicle exactly.

M5 is a strong discrete approximation, but its 15 prototypes still have 20.1\%
higher mean free-run error than M4's three continuous LPV families.  M4 is better
than M5 for every fleet seed, with improvements of 16.4--24.8\%.  Their worst
trajectory errors are similar, indicating that rare within-family deviations,
rather than speed discretization alone, determine the remaining tail.

Experiment 2B therefore passes all gates.  The result establishes the central
oracle claim on the linear plant:
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\text{LPV handles operating-point variation, while specialization
 handles persistent fleet variation.}}
\end{equation}
Controller synthesis may now proceed without changing the validated model
architecture.  The later nonlinear-plant layer remains necessary to determine
whether this conclusion survives tire saturation and model mismatch.
