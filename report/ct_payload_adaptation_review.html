Centered payload adaptation:
local identification leaves little room for a fleet prior

Updating the model helps, particularly the sideslip estimate. But four seconds of local yaw-only data recover almost all of the available benefit in this controlled scenario.

Decision for this scenario

Do not build a fleet prior for the present centered-loading test. At four seconds, the earliest tested prefix, local identification closes 99.4% of the mean yaw-tracking gap and 99.8% of the mean sideslip-estimation gap between the previous model and the known-change reference. Longer prefixes provide virtually no additional control benefit.

This rules out a useful persistent adaptation gap in these tests. It does not rule out a benefit before four seconds, with less informative driving, or under unknown geometry and multiple simultaneous changes. Those would require a separately motivated experiment.

What was tested

Nine previously identified vehicles from one pilot fleet; three centered payload amounts (10%, 20%, 30%); five nested data prefixes (4, 8, 16, 32, 40 s); three held-out speeds (13, 21, 29 m/s). The 27 loading cases are repeated conditions on nine vehicles. There are 135 scalar fits and 567 closed-loop tests, with paired yaw noise across methods.

Previous: saved pre-change model, controller and observer. KnownChange: true loading fraction applied to the same imperfect identified model. Local4 / Local40: loading fraction estimated from four / forty seconds of measured yaw, known speed and applied steering. No measured sideslip enters the new identification or feedback. KnownChange is a reference, not an exact-plant oracle or guaranteed lower bound.

All methods know the change is centered loading with a 1 m payload radius of gyration; only its amount is unknown to Local. Tire stiffness, friction and geometry remain fixed. Acquisition uses the previous controller/observer at 20 m/s and smooth prescribed curves with nominal lateral demand 1.5 m/s². Four seconds cover the first positive lobe of an eight-second left–right curve.

The load is applied before straight-running initialization. Controllers are tested after fitting, in separate reset rollouts. These results do not measure online cumulative tracking performance during adaptation, estimator execution latency, or ordinary unplanned driving. No fleet prior is trained here.

No persistent tracking gap is hidden by the average

All 27 vehicle–loading combinations improve on Previous when averaged over the three speeds. At the individual speed level, Local4 improves yaw RMSE in 68 of 81 cases; small regressions remain. The largest positive Local4–KnownChange difference is 0.00034715°/s. All 27 loading cases satisfy each prespecified descriptive tolerance (0.005, 0.01, 0.02°/s) from four seconds onward at all three speeds, including every later prefix. These tolerances are not vehicle acceptance standards.

Sideslip estimation is less uniform than the tracking averages suggest. Local4 improves it in 78 of 81 cases. Its largest excess RMSE over KnownChange is 0.01184° (heavy_00, 30% loading, 13 m/s: 0.02503° versus 0.01319°), still far below Previous at 0.20299°. In the three handling_01 / 10% loading cases, both updated models estimate sideslip worse than Previous. Thus nearly complete mean gap closure does not mean identical or universally better state estimates.

Integrity and operating checks

All 27 acquisition cases pass the prescribed checks. All 135 fits converge without a boundary solution. All 567 control tests finish and satisfy the defined operating bounds, with no steering-limiter activation and no unstable true-origin linearization. These finite tests and local linearizations are not a global stability or vehicle-safety guarantee.

The review verifies all checkpoint identities, paired control-noise hashes within all 81 vehicle–loading–speed comparisons, all 567 parameter transforms, all aggregate tables, all 135 selected training losses and 5,535 grid losses. It reconstructs all 27 acquisition traces and seven prespecified control previews without refitting. Of 567 regenerated control-noise hashes, 560 match exactly. The remaining seven share one vehicle–loading–speed condition; all seven methods were additionally replayed and their saved metrics checked numerically. The cause of this cross-platform hash difference is not established by this audit. Maximum objective discrepancy: 2.84e-13; maximum replay metric discrepancy: 6.36e-13; maximum preview sample discrepancy: 9.77e-15.

What this means for the research direction

The strong physical model and a single well-excited unknown make this loading change locally identifiable very quickly. A donor prior would have to outperform this baseline over an earlier, practically important window; the present results give no evidence that such a window matters.

Keep this as a negative diagnostic for centered payload adaptation. Do not make coverage or noise artificially harsher just to obtain a learning advantage. If pursuing the broader adaptation direction, first establish a real use case for unknown change structure or weak excitation. Alternatively, discuss a specific missing-physics problem with a correspondingly strong augmented local physical baseline before implementing another experiment.
