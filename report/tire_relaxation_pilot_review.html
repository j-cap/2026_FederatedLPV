Better identification and observation,
but worse yaw tracking with the present design

The four-state model captures the missing force dynamics. A local yaw-only fit already provides accurate predictions. Its current LQI/observer implementation does not improve the control task.

Decision

Keep the extension as a validated pilot model, but do not expand to a federated study on the strength of these results. JointCT4 beats LocalCT2 in all 30 paired prediction cases and loses in all 30 paired control cases. ExactCT4 tracks almost identically to JointCT4, so a better parameter estimate is unlikely to remove the current tracking disadvantage.

The next unresolved issue is the controller/observer design, not additional plant complexity. If we continue this direction, freeze identification and first diagnose the reference response and measurement-noise response separately. This review contains analytical transfer calculations of the saved designs, not new noise ablations, tuning runs or experiments.

1. All planned cases completed

All 27 multistart fits and all 12 additional profile fits converged. All 180 prediction cases and 180 control cases completed; all control cases met the declared sideslip and lateral-acceleration limits. Maximum sideslip was 0.403° and maximum lateral acceleration 1.172 m/s². These remain gentle fixed-speed simulation tests.

Three selected vehicles are not independent population confirmation. The 17 and 25 m/s tests are interpolation between training speeds 13, 21 and 29 m/s; there is no test of incomplete fleet coverage or out-of-range extrapolation here.

The least visible normalized log-parameter direction has absolute μ component above 0.999 for every vehicle. Therefore these data support useful dynamic models, not reliable recovery of all six physical parameters. All LocalCT2 fits also hit the μ upper bound, while their other CT ratios shift substantially to compensate for missing force dynamics.

Fixing a relaxation length at ±25% of its selected value increases weighted SSE by 44–331 after local reoptimization of the other five parameters. No sampled profile point improves on the selected solution. These coarse, single-start profiles, some with active bounds, are evidence against a very broad local valley at this scale, not globally optimized profiles or calibrated confidence intervals.

3. Prediction improves; control does not

For broadband held-out steering, LocalCT2 → JointCT4 reduces mean clean-yaw prediction RMSE from 0.04720 to 0.001484 deg/s (96.9%). For slow steering, it falls from 0.01276 to 0.001171 deg/s (90.8%). Improvement occurs at both observed speeds and intermediate speeds. ExactCT4 gives zero clean prediction error by construction because it uses the generating model and integrator.

Dynamic-reference tracking instead worsens from 0.06808 to 0.09440 deg/s (+38.7%), and slow-reference tracking from 0.01633 to 0.02527 deg/s (+54.7%). The absolute increases are 0.02632 and 0.00894 deg/s. PreviousCT2 also tracks better than JointCT4 in every matched case.

ExactCT4 gives 0.09349 and 0.02502 deg/s: JointCT4 is only about 1% worse. Thus the fitted four-state model already reproduces the known-parameter four-state design's behavior closely. The exact model is a parameter reference, not a guaranteed optimum for yaw tracking under this LQI cost and observer rule.

Across both probes, JointCT4 lowers mean sideslip-estimation RMSE from 0.05765° (LocalCT2) to 0.001726°. Actual sideslip magnitudes are similar; the large difference is observer accuracy. Mean steering RMS increases from 0.4183° to 0.4703°.

All remain within the declared operating bounds. Low limiter fractions should not hide the much higher steering activity. The original 0.1 s preview traces undersample the 0.01 s control sequence and should not be used alone to assess this activity.

JointCT4 has more phase lag (about −1.75° versus −0.86° for LocalCT2) and slightly more amplitude overshoot. Its predicted stationary yaw component due to measurement noise is also higher: 0.02010 versus 0.01198 deg/s. The instantaneous steering response to a yaw-noise increment is approximately −4.49 versus −0.65 rad/rad.

These calculations identify two plausible contributors: the reference transfer and noise amplification both change. They use the true linearized plant, saved model-based gain, steady Kalman gain, independent white yaw noise and inactive limits. Startup, nonlinear tire behavior, tapering and rate limiting are omitted. They therefore do not assign exact portions of the nonlinear tracking error or prove that changing only the observer resolves the result.

The common LQI weights do not define a minimum-yaw-RMSE controller across model orders, and the augmented observer adds an untuned force-process covariance. A more accurate model need not win this particular controller comparison automatically. No control weights or covariances were selected using these test results.

6. Implications for the paper direction

The extension addresses the earlier model–plant match objection: the refitted instantaneous model cannot explain the transient yaw records nearly as well. However, the richer model is already learned accurately by each vehicle with these three-speed records, and it brings no yaw-tracking improvement under the present design. The experiment therefore establishes neither a control advantage nor a need for fleet learning.

I would stop enlarging the plant and fleet for now. If we pursue the control route, use one bounded controller diagnosis with the saved models: separate deterministic reference tracking from measurement-noise response, then decide whether a common, practically justified controller/observer design criterion is needed. If that fails to show a useful advantage, reconsider the task rather than manufacture more model mismatch.
