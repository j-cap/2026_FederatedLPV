Personalized Federated Identification of Vehicle Lateral Dynamics from Limited Local Data

Can a vehicle improve predictions beyond its locally observed speed by learning a physical parameter prior from other vehicles, and how much does this add beyond a fixed nominal prior?

Working paper framing based on the completed development experiments and reviewed figure outputs. This document supports a scope decision; it is not a submission-ready manuscript or a claim of established methodological novelty.

1. From the practical problem to a testable question

A lateral-dynamics model should predict how a particular vehicle responds to steering across the speeds at which it operates. Vehicle parameters differ, while collecting informative identification records across that entire range can require additional testing. We therefore study a restricted-data scenario: each vehicle supplies one short record at one speed, and its model is evaluated at other speeds.

Physical structure already provides a substantial part of the answer. A continuous-time vehicle model contains an explicit speed dependence, so missing local speeds do not automatically imply that the model cannot predict there. The remaining question is whether a noisy, short local record determines the physical parameters accurately enough, and whether other vehicles supply useful information beyond an engineering nominal prior.

Desired capability. Improve predictions for a vehicle with limited local data while preserving its own dynamics, keeping measured trajectories local, and avoiding additional identification records for that target.

Our proposed solution uses personalized physical models and a shared learned regularization center. The essential comparison is against a local estimator with a fixed nominal prior. A second, matched comparison changes only the operating speeds of peers to determine whether complementary coverage contributes further.

Claim supported by the present pilots. In the tested limited-data regime, personalized fleet-learned regularization modestly improves mean yaw prediction accuracy beyond a fixed nominal prior. Complementary peer speeds provide an additional benefit concentrated in low-speed targets.

Limited local data are an explicit scenario assumption here, not an established restriction of all practical vehicles. The current results address identification; a meaningful closed-loop improvement has not been established.

2. Physical structure provides the speed dependence

The current plant is a four-state bicycle model with tanh tire forces and first-order front/rear force relaxation. For vehicle i, β is sideslip, r is yaw rate, and qf, qr are lateral tire forces divided by vehicle mass. Here αf, αr are the model's small-angle slip expressions, af, ar are force-per-mass saturation levels, and qf★, qr★ are equilibrium tire responses. Relaxation supplies the two additional dynamic states. The model identifies physical ratios, not absolute mass, inertia and force scale separately.

Only yaw rate is observed during identification; steering and speed are known. Sideslip and force states are simulated internally. Every record begins from the known zero state in this simulation study. No state reconstruction or unknown-initial-state estimation is part of the present experiment.

This gives a speed-dependent physical model through a compact parameter vector. It changes the earlier paper emphasis: the current experiment learns parameters of these equations, rather than independent matrix entries or a flexible polynomial LPV basis. Both identification and test records have fixed speed; variable-speed and longitudinal dynamics are outside this study.

What is actually federated?

Each local endpoint owns its yaw/input record and solves its own nonlinear problem. Per round, it uploads six log parameters and one scalar data loss, and receives six center coordinates: 13 float64 values, or 104 numerical payload bytes per client-round, before network headers and excluding initialization. The scalar losses support objective and convergence accounting. The experiment emulates this message interface on one machine. It establishes neither a deployed distributed implementation nor a privacy guarantee.

4. A matched experiment separates three effects

The three development fleets each contain nine vehicle realizations drawn from nominal, heavy and handling families. Each family contributes one target at 13, 21 and 29 m/s. Family labels and generating parameters are used for simulation/evaluation, not supplied to the learner. Every target provides one 12 s record, sampled at 0.01 s: 1200 steering intervals and 1201 yaw samples. Integration uses RK4 with 0.001 s internal steps.

The prescribed steering is a tapered multisine with a 0.35° peak. Training frequencies are 0.35, 0.7 and 1.2 Hz; evaluation uses distinct slow and broadband probes with different frequencies and phases. Steering is known without measurement noise; only the yaw observations are noisy. These are gentle synthetic identification maneuvers, not recorded road-driving trajectories.

For a target at 13 m/s, the same-speed condition places all eight peers at 13 m/s. The complementary condition keeps two peers at 13 m/s and places three at 21 m/s and three at 29 m/s. The corresponding permutation applies to the other target speeds. The target record, peer identities, input signals, paired noise samples, data duration and fitting settings are held fixed. Only six peers' operating speeds change. Each fitted fleet condition uses nine records, totaling 108 s; no target gains its own other-speed training data.

Primary evaluation averages clean yaw prediction RMSE over the other two anchor speeds, using both probes. The indices v and p below identify evaluation speed and probe. Errors are computed in radians per second and reported in degrees per second:

There are four primary cases per vehicle, 36 per fleet, and 108 across 27 vehicles. Means first average matched cases and are equally weighted across vehicles/fleets in this balanced design. Percentage gains are ratios of mean errors. The 108 trajectories do not constitute 108 independent fleet replications. Additional speeds 9, 17, 25 and 33 m/s are available as secondary diagnostics.

All nine same-speed fits and all three complementary fits now meet the original stopping criteria. The two previously incomplete same-speed fits ended at rounds 32 and 46; completing them changed the pooled peer-speed benefit from 4.30% to 4.10%.

Complementary peer speeds mainly help low-speed targets

Compared with same-speed peers, complementary peers reduce mean error by 4.10%, or 0.000169 deg/s. The effect depends on the target's training speed: a 6.79% improvement at 13 m/s, a 0.13% deterioration at 21 m/s, and a 1.63% deterioration at 29 m/s. In the low-speed stratum, fleet-specific gains range from 0.65% to 19.07%.

This supports a conditional benefit from the tested peer-speed intervention. Changing speed also changes the yaw response amplitude, parameter sensitivity and output signal-to-noise ratio for the same steering. The experiment does not isolate geometric coverage independently of those effects.

6. What the paper can claim, and what remains open

The result is consistent with the following interpretation: physical structure makes prediction beyond a local speed possible, a prior substantially improves short-record estimation, and a fleet-learned prior can add a modest population-dependent improvement. Complementary operating speeds supply a further benefit for some low-speed targets. This interpretation is supported by matched comparisons; it is not a proof of parameter recovery or a causal decomposition of the total gain.

Proposed paper contributions

  1. Formulation: personalized yaw-only physical identification with data-local nonlinear OE and a shared log-parameter regularizer.
  2. Evaluation design: comparisons that separately assess regularization, learning the prior, and changing peer operating speeds while preserving target data and peer identities.
  3. Empirical finding: a qualified benefit over a strong fixed-prior baseline, with explicit conditions under which complementary peers help and cases where they do not.

The formulation should not be presented as a new general FL algorithm on the strength of these experiments. Its distinction from existing personalized physical identification and regularized multi-task learning requires a focused literature assessment before settling a novelty claim.

Limits that materially affect interpretation

  • Three reused development fleets. This is exploratory evidence; there is no independent confirmation for the new physical-prior story.
  • Favorable shared structure. All vehicles use known geometry 1.2/1.6 m and true relaxation lengths 0.4/0.7 m. The nominal prior uses 0.6/0.6 m. A common true dynamic property creates an opportunity for sharing; this does not establish transfer between vehicles with different relaxation lengths.
  • Matched physical model class. Both the plant and estimator use the same four-state tanh/relaxation structure. This is not a demonstration under unmodeled dynamics or measured-road data.
  • One prescribed nominal prior and weight matrix. Beating this baseline does not imply superiority to every locally selected physical prior or regularization strategy.
  • Known zero initial state and fixed-speed tests. General operation with unknown initial force/sideslip states is not tested here.
  • Prediction is the endpoint. The earlier four-state controller/observer pilot did not establish better yaw tracking; the present peer comparison adds no new closed-loop evidence.
Earlier Global, grouped and control results

The initial limited-speed pilot found poor prediction from a single shared Global model, and the tested grouped variant did not outperform the single learned prior. Those experiments motivate the personalized formulation, but they are not added to the final four-method figure as though all optimization protocols were equally closed. The earlier four-state prediction/control pilot also used a different protocol and should not be pooled with these errors. See the preserved limited-speed audit and four-state control audit for their evidence and limits.

Decisions for the discussion with Markus

  1. Is a conditional identification benefit beyond a strong physical prior substantial enough to be the paper's central contribution?
  2. Is the assumed local-data restriction credible for the intended use case, and how should we state it without implying a demonstrated real-world constraint?
  3. Should the intended paper be identification-focused, or should a meaningful closed-loop benefit remain a requirement before pursuing a control-paper submission?
  4. Which genuinely new element distinguishes the formulation or evidence from established regularized/personalized identification?

Recommended scope decision. Use this document to agree on the question and required level of evidence before preparing further experiments or rewriting the manuscript. The current evidence supports a qualified identification story; it does not yet support a strong claim that FL materially improves vehicle control.
